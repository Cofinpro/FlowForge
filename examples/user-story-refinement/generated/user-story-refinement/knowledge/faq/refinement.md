## refinement-1: How are Three Amigos, Given-When-Then, example tables, planning poker and vertical splitting done, and when is a story sprint-sized?

- asked 2026-09-26 · notebook "The Product - Business Design" · conversation `c25c0d30-1c0c-44bd-a4a5-4096acbab763`
- Sources: Kelly, The Art of Agile Product Ownership (2019); Adzic et al., Fifty Quick Ideas to Improve Your User Stories (2014); Patton, User Story Mapping; Cohn, User Stories Applied (2004); Bland, Testing Business Ideas (2019); Olsen, The Lean Product Playbook; Richter, The Product Manager's Playbook (2023); Gothelf & Seiden, Lean UX; Maurya, Running Lean

**Question**

> Context: a running IT delivery project; a new user story (Connextra format, INVEST self-checked) now goes into refinement. Our refinement phase is: schedule a Three Amigos refinement (PO/BA, development, QA; UX when needed) -> in parallel: PO explains value and business rules (the 'what'), development clarifies implementation approach and dependencies (the 'how'), QA identifies edge cases and negative scenarios, UX attaches wireframes and interaction flow -> QA writes acceptance criteria as Given-When-Then scenarios -> add example tables (Specification by Example) -> planning poker estimate (human step) -> gateway 'estimates converged?' (no -> discuss and re-estimate) -> gateway 'does the story fit into one sprint?' (no -> split vertically with splitting patterns, then reformulate the part stories). Based on the sources: (a) What is the recommended procedure and output for each of these steps (Three Amigos, Given-When-Then, example tables, planning poker)? (b) What are the criteria for 'estimates converged' and for 'fits into a sprint' (size limits, how many re-estimation rounds)? (c) Which vertical splitting patterns do the sources list, with a one-line description each? (d) Which steps or checks do the sources describe for refinement that this process lacks, and which pitfalls do they name? Please cite the sources.

**Answer** (verbatim, citation markers resolve in the table below)

Hier is a detailed, source-grounded breakdown addressing each part of your refinement process.

---

##### (a) Recommended Procedure and Output for Refinement Steps

###### 1. Three Amigos
* **Procedure:** The Three Amigos session brings together representatives of three core perspectives: **Business/Product** (Product Owner or Business Analyst explaining intent and value), **Development** (engineers probing technical implementation, architecture, and dependencies), and **Testing** (QA applying testing heuristics to surface edge cases and negative scenarios) [1-6]. If user interactions or domain details are involved, **UX designers** or domain experts participate as a fourth or fifth amigo [6-8]. Crucially, the meeting **must involve the actual developers and testers who will deliver the story**, rather than delegates, to prevent knowledge loss [9].
* **Output:** A shared mental model across roles [10], clarified scope and business rules [1, 11], initial agreed-upon test scenarios [1, 3], and identified functional risks or missing requirements [3].

###### 2. Given-When-Then (Scenarios / Acceptance Criteria)
* **Procedure:** Acceptance criteria are specified as executable behavior scenarios (BDD / Specification by Example format) [2, 12]. The scenario outlines the preconditions (**Given**), the triggering user action or event (**When**), and the verifiable system response (**Then**) [12, 13]. These scenarios are defined collaboratively prior to coding to align expectations and establish unambiguous bounds for when the story is "Done" [14-16].
* **Output:** Precise, testable acceptance criteria written on or linked to the story card [15-17] that capture business intent and serve as the blueprint for automated acceptance tests [12, 18, 19].

###### 3. Example Tables (Specification by Example / SBE)
* **Procedure:** Broad scenario rules are instantiated into concrete data tables (e.g., FIT / FitNesse format) [12, 16, 20]. Each row represents a specific test case with concrete input parameters and expected outcome values (such as `true`/`false` validity checks) [12, 20].
* **Output:** Structured example tables [12, 20]. These serve both as unambiguous business documentation and as automated, executable acceptance test suites that developers can run against the codebase [12, 20].

###### 4. Planning Poker
* **Procedure:** The team estimates relative effort using a predefined card scale (often Fibonacci numbers: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13...) [21, 22]. The PO reads the story and answers questions [23, 24]. All estimators privately select a card and reveal simultaneously [22, 23]. If there is significant divergence, the highest and lowest estimators explain their reasoning without judgment [22, 23, 25]. The group briefly discusses technical assumptions (e.g., database choices or mock objects) [24, 25] and votes again [24, 26].
* **Output:** A single converged numerical story point score representing the relative effort and complexity of the story [21, 22, 26, 27].

---

##### (b) Criteria for 'Estimates Converged' and 'Fits into a Sprint'

###### Criteria for "Estimates Converged"
* **Convergence Rule:** Unanimous card alignment is **not strictly necessary** [27]. Convergence is reached when there is a narrow consensus or when outliers agree to the group's majority estimate (e.g., three voting 4 and one voting 3 points can settle on 4) [27]. Reasonableness beats absolute precision [27, 28].
* **Re-estimation Rounds:** Poker estimation rarely takes more than **2 to 3 rounds** [26]. Continuing beyond 3 rounds is recommended only as long as estimates are actively moving closer together [26, 27]. Debating minor point differences on large estimates (e.g., 7 vs. 8) is discouraged as it implies false precision [28].

###### Criteria for "Fits into a Sprint"
* **Ideal Size Limit:** An individual user story should ideally take a developer between **1 to 5 ideal days** to complete [29].
* **Sprint Capacity Constraint:** A story must fit entirely within a single iteration velocity [30, 31]; teams are not permitted to deliver half a feature within a sprint [32].
* **Splitting Trigger:** If a story exceeds the 1–5 ideal day range, or if its story point estimate consumes an oversized fraction of the sprint velocity, it fails the "fits into a sprint" gate and must be split [33-35].

---

##### (c) Vertical Story Splitting Patterns listed in the Sources

1. **Split by Business Rules / Data Variations:** Slicing a compound story by specific business rules or data variations (e.g., supporting Visa first, then adding MasterCard and Amex in follow-up stories) [33, 36].
2. **Split by Operations / CRUD Boundaries:** Breaking down broad functionality into discrete user operations (e.g., Create vs. View vs. Edit vs. Delete) [37-39].
3. **Split by Input Channels / Data Sources:** Simplifying inputs by using local or hard-coded reference data (like flat files or Excel) first, before connecting to live or external database channels [40].
4. **Split by Output Formats / Output Channels:** Delivering the simplest output format first (e.g., plain text or Excel) before building complex exports (PDF, data warehouse integrations) [41-43].
5. **Split by Workflow Steps (Hamburger Slicing):** Slicing horizontally across sequential steps in a user workflow (e.g., assemble, send, report) to create a thin, end-to-end operational slice [44].
6. **Split by Examples of Usefulness:** Slicing out a few specific, valuable usage examples that depend on only a small portion of the overall technical architecture [45, 46].
7. **Split by Spike (Research vs. Implementation):** Separating an unestimatable or complex technical investigation into a timeboxed spike story, followed by the feature implementation story in subsequent iterations [37, 47-49].
8. **Split by Priority (Core vs. Enhancement):** Stripping out secondary enhancements or "nice-to-have" capabilities to leave only the high-priority core functionality [35, 36, 38].

---

##### (d) Missing Steps, Checks, and Pitfalls in the Refinement Process

###### Missing Refinement Steps and Checks
* **Discovery Backlog Gate / Problem Validation:** The described process starts directly with delivery refinement. Sources recommend an **Opportunity/Discovery Backlog** step prior to the Delivery Backlog to validate problem relevance and alignment before refinement begins [50-53].
* **Post-Delivery Outcome Validation (Feedback Loop):** Refinement does not end at deployment. Sources emphasize measuring live feature outcomes and qualitative user feedback after release to feed back into discovery and backlog prioritization [54-57].
* **Backlog Pruning & Weeding:** Refinement should include actively deleting, deprioritizing, or discarding expired or valueless stories to prevent backlog inflation [11, 58, 59].
* **Explicit Handling of Non-Functional Requirements (NFRs):** The process lacks explicit checks for NFRs or constraints (e.g., performance intervals using the QUPER model or security constraints), which should be tracked as Constraint cards or automated tests rather than forced into story templates [60-63].
* **Upfront Stakeholder Alignment on Thin Slices:** Communicating the purpose of simplified first cuts (e.g., basic UI or temporary files) to business stakeholders beforehand, preventing dissatisfaction over missing advanced polish in early versions [64].
* **Post-Delivery Story Disposal:** Story cards should be discarded after completion rather than maintained as permanent documentation [65, 66].

###### Refinement Pitfalls Named by Sources
* **Delegating 3-Amigos to Non-Delivery Personnel:** Assigning pre-analysis to team members who will not actually build or test the story causes critical knowledge-transfer breakdowns ("telephone game") during iteration planning [9].
* **Over-Refining & Thinking Too Far Ahead:** Spending excessive time detailing stories far in advance rather than conducting just-in-time refinement [67-69].
* **Template Zombies & Fake Stories:** Forcing pure technical tasks or simple infrastructure changes into rigid user story templates leads to artificial roles (e.g., "As an LDAP server...") or solution-disguised tickets [13, 53, 70].
* **Technical / Horizontal Layer Splitting:** Developers splitting stories along architectural layers (e.g., "build database schema" vs. "build UI"), resulting in slices that deliver no standalone user value [34, 45].
* **Using Stories as Permanent Documentation:** Attaching long-term specs and test cases to completed story tickets, making system understanding error-prone and labor-intensive over time [65, 66].
* **Goldplating:** Adding unrequested technical features or unneeded scope during implementation because story boundaries were vague [71].
* **Splitting Too Frequently During Planning:** Frequently needing to split stories during sprint planning indicates that backlog refinement was neglected beforehand [35, 72].
