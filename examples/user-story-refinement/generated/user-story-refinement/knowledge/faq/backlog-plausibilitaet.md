## backlog-plausibilitaet-1: How is a ready story checked against the rest of the backlog, merged or marked, and prioritised?

- asked 2026-09-26 · notebook "The Product - Business Design" · conversation `14cf6b3c-6a40-4a98-90f2-d35e20b071cc`
- Sources: Cohn, User Stories Applied (2004); Adzic et al., Fifty Quick Ideas to Improve Your User Stories (2014); Kelly, The Art of Agile Product Ownership (2019); Pohl & Rupp, Basiswissen Requirements Engineering (2015); Olsen, The Lean Product Playbook; Patton, User Story Mapping; Anforderungsverwaltung.pdf; Richter, The Product Manager's Playbook (2023); Humble et al., Lean Enterprise

**Question**

> Context: a running IT delivery project; a new, ready user story is checked for plausibility against the rest of the product backlog before release. Our last phase is: in parallel: PO checks duplicates and overlaps, UX checks consistency with existing interaction flows, development checks dependencies and ordering, QA checks the acceptance criteria against other stories and the living documentation -> merge the four findings into one plausibility report -> gateway 'consistent with the rest of the backlog?' (contradiction -> back to refinement; duplicate -> merge into the existing story, end) -> mark affected stories for adjustment -> prioritise the story and move it to the Ready column (human step, PO) -> story is sprint-ready. Based on the sources: (a) Which steps, checks or roles do the sources describe for this phase that the process lacks or does differently? (b) How should a duplicate be merged into an existing story, and how are affected or outdated stories marked? (c) Which prioritisation method do the sources recommend for placing a new feedback-driven story in the backlog (e.g. value vs. effort, cost of delay, WSJF), with the inputs it needs? (d) Which pitfalls do the sources name for this phase? Please cite the sources.

**Answer** (verbatim, citation markers resolve in the table below)

Based on the provided sources, here is a detailed analysis comparing your described backlog check process against agile and requirements engineering frameworks:

---

##### (a) Missing Steps, Checks, or Roles
While your process covers technical, UX, QA, and PO perspectives, the sources highlight several structural differences and missing elements:

*   **Collaborative Refinement vs. Hand-over Documentation:** Your process relies on four roles working in parallel to write a consolidated "plausibility report." The sources strongly warn that user stories are **tokens for a conversation**, not detailed written specifications [1, 2]. Replacing direct discussions with siloed parallel reviews and written reports reintroduces waterfall-style hand-overs, creates a documentation bottleneck, and loses the benefits of cross-functional set-based design (e.g., **3-Amigos** or diverge-and-merge team discussions) [2-5].
*   **Independent Review Principle:** In requirements engineering (IREB), a core quality principle is the **independence of the reviewer** [6]. In your process, the PO checks their own backlog items for duplicates, whereas quality assurance principles state that authors should not review their own artifacts due to confirmation bias [6].
*   **Team Capacity & Commitment for "Sprint-Ready" Status:** Moving a story to the *Ready* column in your process is described purely as a "human step, PO." In frameworks like Scrum and Kanban, a story becomes sprint-ready only when the **entire development team** has jointly estimated its effort/complexity (e.g., via Story Points) and agreed that it meets the team's *Definition of Ready* (such as the INVEST criteria) [7-10].
*   **Handling Unclear Technical Dependencies via Spikes:** When development identifies high technical uncertainty or dependency risks during the check, the sources recommend creating a **Spike or Learning Story** (a time-boxed research story) to resolve technical risk before making the functional story sprint-ready [11, 12].
*   **Decoupling Living Documentation from Stories:** While your QA checks acceptance criteria against living documentation, the sources caution against maintaining documentation *by story* [13, 14]. Once implemented, acceptance criteria and specifications should be restructured by **functional area** rather than tied permanently to story tickets [15, 16].

---

##### (b) Merging Duplicates and Marking Affected or Outdated Stories

###### Merging Duplicate Stories
*   **Combining and Bundling:** When duplicate or overlapping stories are identified, they should be merged into a single larger story card [17-19]. 
*   **Distillation / Aggregation:** Jeff Patton (*User Story Mapping*) and Gojko Adzic recommend taking a cluster of overlapping stories, writing their titles as bullet points under a single new "header" or "distillation" story card, and adjusting the combined estimate [20, 21].
*   **Stapling Small Items:** For tiny duplicate items or small bug reports, Extreme Programming practices suggest stapling them together with a cover card to be estimated and scheduled as one unit [17, 19, 22].

###### Marking Affected and Outdated Stories
*   **Impact Analysis & Traceability:** Requirements management standards prescribe using **Traceability** links to conduct an *impact analysis* (*Auswirkungsanalyse*) [23, 24]. Affected stories and upstream/downstream artifacts are updated to a new version (e.g., incrementing from v1.0 to v1.1) or grouped into new baseline configurations [25-27].
*   **Archiving / "Trash" State:** For unneeded, invalid, or duplicate stories in digital backlogs, items should be explicitly moved to a **Trash / Rejected** state with a recorded reason [28, 29] or categorized as "Won't have" in MoSCoW [30].
*   **Throwing Away Completed Stories:** Once a story is delivered, the sources recommend **throwing the story away** (closing/deleting the card) rather than keeping old stories as a perpetual change log [16]. The system's current behavior should live in functionally organized documentation, not in historical story tickets [13, 16].
*   **Expiry Dates ("Best Before"):** Time-sensitive stories should be explicitly tagged with an **expiry / "best before" date** so the team can manage them before they turn into emergencies or clutter the backlog [31, 32].

---

##### (c) Recommended Prioritisation Method for Feedback-Driven Stories

For continuously changing, feedback-driven backlogs, the sources strongly advocate for **Weighted Shortest Job First (WSJF)** driven by **Cost of Delay (CoD)** [33-37].

\\[\text{WSJF} = \frac{\text{Cost of Delay (CoD)}}{\text{Job Size (Aufwand / Story Points)}}\\] [34, 38]

###### Required Inputs:
1.  **Cost of Delay (CoD)**:
    *   **User Value & Business Value:** What benefit does the story deliver to end users versus the business? [34]
    *   **Time Criticality:** How rapidly does the value decay over time, or are there hard deadlines? [38, 39]
    *   **Risk Reduction & Opportunity Enablement:** Does the story mitigate future risk or unlock new business options? [38]
2.  **Job Size (Effort):**
    *   Estimated development effort/complexity, typically provided by the development team in relative **Story Points** or relative duration [38, 40].

###### Alternative / Complementary Methods in the Sources:
*   **Kano Model:** Classifies feedback into *Basisfaktoren* (must-haves), *Leistungsfaktoren* (satisfiers), and *Begeisterungsfaktoren* (delighters) to evaluate how fulfilling the feature impacts user satisfaction [41-43].
*   **Wiegers’ Prioritisation Matrix:** Evaluates relative value, relative penalty, estimated cost, and relative risk to calculate a priority score [42].
*   **Value vs. Cost Matrix:** Comparing customer/purchaser desirability against developer estimates [8, 44, 45].

---

##### (d) Pitfalls Named in the Sources for This Phase

*   **The "Fuzzy Front End" Bottleneck:** Holding items in lengthy analysis and report-building queues before development creates massive lead-time waste (e.g., weeks spent waiting in queues for work that takes hours to code) [46, 47].
*   **Fake User Stories & Technical Tasks as Stories:** Turning technical infrastructure, refactoring, or QA tasks into fake stories (e.g., *"As a QA, I want automated logs..."* or *"As a developer..."*) [48-50]. Technical tasks always lose in priority against user-value stories until systems break [51]; they should be managed via dedicated time budgets rather than competing as user stories [52].
*   **Generic User Roles:** Allowing vague roles like *"As a user..."* or *"As an API"* encourages stakeholders to push pet features without validating specific target user segments [53, 54].
*   **"Stream of Consciousness" Backlog:** Passively accepting all stakeholder feedback into the backlog without strict triage, leading to a bloated backlog full of outdated or expired ("rotten fruit") items [31, 53, 55].
*   **Using Stories as Permanent Documentation:** Trying to understand current system behavior by reading past user stories in reverse chronological order, which becomes error-prone and unsustainable over time [13, 14].
*   **HiPPO Prioritisation:** Relying on the *Highest Paid Person's Opinion* or subjective gut feel rather than transparent economic metrics like Cost of Delay [56, 57].
*   **Goldplating & Solutioning:** Specifying technical solutions instead of underlying user needs, or developers adding unrequested enhancements during execution [58, 59].
