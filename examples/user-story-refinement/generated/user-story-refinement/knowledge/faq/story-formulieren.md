## story-formulieren-1: How is a feedback-driven story placed in the story map, written in Connextra format and checked with INVEST?

- asked 2026-09-26 · notebook "The Product - Business Design" · conversation `fe647ce8-c4cc-45ff-92a6-eac1d2255aea`
- Sources: Adzic et al., Fifty Quick Ideas to Improve Your User Stories (2014); Kalbach, Mapping Experiences (2020); Patton, User Story Mapping; Cohn, User Stories Applied (2004); Olsen, The Lean Product Playbook; Kelly, The Art of Agile Product Ownership (2019); Anforderungsverwaltung.pdf; Pohl & Rupp, Basiswissen Requirements Engineering (2015); Angrave, The Journey Mapping Playbook (2020); Humble et al., Lean Enterprise; Lewrick et al., The Design Thinking Toolbox (2020); Anforderungsdokumentation.pdf

**Question**

> Context: a running IT delivery project with existing product vision, epics, Fachkonzept (business concept with domain model) and backlog; a new story comes from user or stakeholder feedback and has already been clarified. Our phase 'formulate the story' is: place the story under its epic in the user story map (backbone of activities and user tasks) -> write it in Connextra format ('As a [role] I want [goal] so that [benefit]') using the user role from the Fachkonzept -> document the link to epic, Fachkonzept and the triggering feedback (traceability vision -> business goal -> epic -> story) -> INVEST self-check (business rule task) -> gateway 'INVEST met?' (no -> reformulate). Based on the sources: (a) What is the step-by-step procedure for placing a story in the user story map and for writing a good Connextra story (who writes it, what the role/goal/benefit parts must contain, common anti-patterns)? (b) Give a concrete INVEST checklist: for each of Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable the pass/fail question and typical failure signs. (c) What does the sources' traceability from vision to story need to contain? (d) Which steps or checks do the sources describe for this phase that the process lacks? Please cite the sources.

**Answer** (verbatim, citation markers resolve in the table below)

##### (a) Step-by-Step Procedure for Story Mapping and Writing Connextra Stories

###### 1. Procedure for Placing a Story in the User Story Map
* **Step 1: Identify the Backbone Activity/Step (Horizontal Axis):** Identify the horizontal narrative flow representing high-level user activities and steps ordered left-to-right from the customer’s perspective [1-4]. These backbone steps must remain technology-agnostic and solution-neutral [5].
* **Step 2: Assign to the Matching Backbone Task/Epic:** Place the story horizontally under the specific user task or epic within the backbone that it directly supports [2, 5, 6].
* **Step 3: Determine Vertical Placement (Slicing & Priority):** Move the story vertically under its backbone step to set its delivery priority across planned releases or milestones [2, 3, 6, 7].
* **Step 4: Explore Options and Thinning:** Treat vertical positioning as a flexible option—explore whether a partial solution, simpler deliverable, or semi-automated initial version can be sliced out to ship value earlier [6, 8, 9].

###### 2. Procedure for Writing a Good Connextra Story
* **Who Writes It:** The **customer team** (Product Owner, Business Analyst, or business stakeholders) holds primary responsibility for writing story cards, as they represent user needs and business value [10-12]. However, it is a joint effort where developers collaborate to ensure stories act as promises for conversation, remain testable, and are properly sized [11, 13, 14].
* **Structure (`As a [role], I want [goal], so that [benefit]`):**
  * **`As a [role]`:** Must reference a specific user role, persona, or concrete customer segment defined in the domain model / *Fachkonzept* [15-19].
  * **`I want [goal]`:** Must specify the desired capability, feature, or deliverable without forcing premature user interface or technical implementation details [17, 19-22].
  * **`So that [benefit]`:** Must articulate the concrete stakeholder value, outcome, or intended behavior change, moving away from generic placeholders [17, 19-21, 23].
* **Common Anti-Patterns:**
  * **Generic Roles ("As a user..."):** Using "As a user" or generic placeholders hides real customer needs, inviting scope creep and pet features [15, 16, 24].
  * **Fake / Developer Stories:** Writing stories for internal delivery team needs (e.g., "As a QA...", refactoring, or database setup) rather than end-user or purchaser value [22, 25].
  * **Template Zombies:** Forcing every requirement into the three-clause Connextra template even when concrete questions or examples spark a far better discussion [20, 26].
  * **Treating Cards as Written Contracts:** Assuming the written card is a formal specification rather than a "placeholder for a conversation" (Ron Jeffries' Card, Conversation, Confirmation) [27-31].
  * **Premature UI/Technical Specification:** Specifying exact screen layouts or backend mechanics on the card upfront rather than refining details collaboratively during story workshops [22, 32].

##### (b) Concrete INVEST Checklist

Bill Wake’s **INVEST** criteria evaluate whether a user story is ready for development [33-35]:

| Criteria | Pass/Fail Evaluation Question | Typical Failure Signs / Red Flags |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Independent (I)** | Can this story be implemented, prioritized, and shipped independently of other stories in any order? [34, 35] | Tightly coupled stories; Story A cannot be coded or tested until Story B is finished; complex sequential scheduling bottlenecks [35]. |
| **Negotiable (N)** | Is the story card an open invitation to converse and negotiate solution details rather than a rigid spec? [12, 28, 30, 34] | Card contains exhaustive UI/technical specs [32]; stakeholders treat card text as a legally binding contract ("it's written right here") [12, 30]. |
| **Valuable (V)** | Does the story deliver explicit, recognizable value to a real end-user, customer, or purchaser? [11, 22, 34] | "Fake stories" benefiting only internal developers/QA [22, 25]; meaningless value statements like "so that business improves" [23]. |
| **Estimable (E)** | Do developers possess enough domain and technical clarity to estimate the effort required? [12, 34] | Developers say "we have no idea how long this takes"; lack of domain knowledge or technical understanding [12]; story is too ambiguous [12, 36]. |
| **Small (S)** | Is the story small enough to be designed, coded, and tested within a single iteration (typically 1–3 days)? [33-36] | Story is an Epic or "big rock" [36, 37]; story point estimate exceeds single-sprint velocity; task spans multiple iterations [35, 37, 38]. |
| **Testable (T)** | Are there objective pass/fail criteria or test cases that prove when the story is done? [28, 34, 39, 40] | Vague/subjective claims like "system must be fast" or "user can quickly master system" [41]; no clear acceptance tests jotted down [39, 40]. |

##### (c) Sources' Traceability Requirements (Vision to Story)

Requirements traceability (*Verfolgbarkeit*) ensures every deliverable remains linked across the entire lifecycle [42-44]:

1. **Pre-RS Traceability (Upstream Origin):** Links the user story directly to its originating trigger, such as specific user feedback, customer interviews, stakeholder requests, or problem hypotheses [42-44].
2. **Goal & Hierarchy Alignment (Vision → Goal → Epic → Story):**
   * **Product Vision & Strategy:** Alignment with the overarching product vision and mission statements [45-47].
   * **Business Goals & Target Outcomes:** Direct mapping to measurable business objectives or target impacts (e.g., via Impact Maps or milestone goals) [21, 48-50].
   * **User Roles / Personas:** Traceability to the specific user roles defined in the *Fachkonzept* / domain model [14, 15, 41, 48].
   * **Epics / Backbone Activities:** Connection to the parent activity and workflow step in the user story map [2, 6, 46, 48].
3. **Core Metadata Attributes:** Each story must carry a unique ID, story name, description, stability score, owner, source/origin, and explicit cross-references to upstream *Fachkonzept* sections [43, 51, 52].
4. **Post-RS Traceability (Downstream Realization):** Links forward from the user story to architectural components, code implementations, and acceptance test cases [42, 44, 53, 54].

##### (d) Missing Steps and Checks in Your Process

Compared to the literature, your current process (`Place on Map → Write Connextra → Link Traceability → INVEST check`) lacks several essential quality gates:

1. **Missing "Confirmation" (Acceptance Criteria & Test Notes):** Your process stops at writing the card description. Under Ron Jeffries’ **3 Cs (Card, Conversation, Confirmation)**, a story is incomplete without explicit acceptance criteria or test notes jotted on the back of the card [28, 29, 31, 40].
2. **Lack of Collaborative "Three Amigos" Workshops:** Your process treats formulation as an isolated self-check. Sources mandate a collaborative **Three Amigos session** (Product Owner/Analyst, Developer, Tester) to probe for edge cases, technical feasibility, and testability before committing [36, 55-58].
3. **Absence of a Demonstration Check:** Asking upfront *"How will we demonstrate this story in the sprint review?"* is a critical check that exposes missing UI flows, missing test data, or unclear scenarios [28, 59].
4. **No Protocol for Unestimable/Risky Stories (Learning vs. Earning):** If INVEST fails on "Estimable" due to technical uncertainty, simply looping to "reformulate" is insufficient. Sources recommend explicitly splitting out a time-boxed **"Learning Story" (Research Spike)** to gather information before attempting an "Earning Story" [60].
5. **No Post-Delivery Behavior Verification:** Stories should include an explicit check to verify post-release whether the expected user behavior change or business outcome actually materialized (Build-Measure-Learn loop) [33, 50, 61, 62].
