## store-phase-1-triage-1: How is feedback captured and formulated as a user problem (I1)?

- asked 2026-10-04 · notebook "The Product - Business Design" · conversation `a7730d21-29f6-4de5-b44e-12ba5f96e611`
- Sources: Bland, Testing Business Ideas (2019); Gothelf & Seiden, Lean UX; Kalbach, Mapping Experiences (2020); Klein, UX for Lean Startups; LeMay, Product Management in Practice (2022); Cohn, User Stories Applied (2004); Kelly, The Art of Agile Product Ownership (2019); Lewrick et al., The Design Thinking Toolbox (2020); Maurya, Running Lean; Croll & Yoskovitz, Lean Analytics; Olsen, The Lean Product Playbook; Product School, The Product Book (2017); Adzic et al., Fifty Quick Ideas to Improve Your User Stories (2014); Patton, User Story Mapping; Humble et al., Lean Enterprise; Angrave, The Journey Mapping Playbook (2020); The Design Thinking Playbook; Wallaert, Start at the End (2019)

**Question**

> Describe the step-by-step procedure for capturing incoming user or stakeholder feedback and formulating it as a user problem (not a solution wish), as the sources document it — what to record about the feedback (source, sender, context), the ordered steps and questions to turn a request into a problem statement or desired behaviour change, and the output artefact. Please cite the sources.

**Answer** (verbatim, citation markers resolve in the table below)

To capture incoming feedback and translate solution wishes into grounded user problems or desired behavior changes, the sources document a structured, step-by-step procedure:

---

##### 1. What to Record About Incoming Feedback

When feedback arrives—whether from support tickets, sales conversations, user interviews, surveys, or stakeholder meetings—you should record three primary dimensions [1-4]:

* **Source & Channel Context**: Record the exact channel where the feedback originated (e.g., support desk calls, community forums, sales reports, user testing sessions, or direct emails) [1-4]. Note the frequency of occurrence and how many users are affected [5, 6].
* **Sender & Stakeholder Profile**: Capture who submitted the feedback, distinguishing end-users from internal proxies, buyers, or managers (who may misrepresent end-user needs) [7-9]. Record their experience level with the product (e.g., beginner vs. power user) and their functional role [7, 10].
* **Raw Qualitative & Observational Data**:
  * **Verbatim Phrasing**: Document exact customer quotes and words rather than paraphrasing them into technical terms [11-13].
  * **Behavioral & Emotional Context**: Record tone of voice, emotional state (e.g., frustration, delight), body language, and perceived urgency [12, 14, 15].
  * **Current Workarounds**: Note how the user is currently coping with or solving the issue today [16-18].

---

##### 2. Ordered Procedure and Questions to Turn Requests into Problem Statements

When users or stakeholders propose a specific feature or "solution wish" (e.g., "Add comments to product pages" or "Build a mobile app"), jumping straight to implementation risks building the wrong feature [19-21]. Instead, follow this ordered procedure:

###### Step 1: Restate the Request Without Solution Commitment
* Acknowledge and restate the feature request to ensure the sender feels heard [22]. 
* Explicitly pause and pivot from the **solution space** (what to build) to the **problem space** (why it matters) [23-25].

###### Step 2: Probe for the Root Cause and Underlying Pain
Apply targeted probing questions to uncover the true friction:
* **The "5x Why" Analysis**: Ask *"Why do you want this feature?"* or *"What would this allow you to do that you can't do today?"* Repeat "Why?" up to 5 times to drill past surface symptoms to the root pain [26-29].
* **The "Magic Wand" Question**: Ask *"If you could wave a magic wand and do anything you can't do today, what would it be?"* This bypasses pre-conceived solution constraints [22, 30].

###### Step 3: Map the Problem Context (5W+H Inquiry)
Gather structural clarity around the problem [31, 32] using the following core questions [33-35]:
1. **What**: *What is the specific problem or friction? Why is it a problem?* [33-35]
2. **Who**: *Who specifically has this problem, and who is affected by it?* [33-35]
3. **When & Where**: *When and where in the user flow or journey does this occur?* [33-35]
4. **How Today**: *How is it being solved or worked around today?* [16, 17, 33, 34]
5. **Why Unsolved**: *Why does it occur, and why hasn't it been solved yet?* [35]

###### Step 4: Reframe as an Outcome or Desired Behavior Change
* Shift focus from **outputs** (shipping features) to **outcomes** (changes in customer behavior) [36-39].
* Highlight contrast by defining the gap between current behavior and desired future behavior using phrasing like *"Whereas currently..."* or *"Instead of..."* [40].
* Quantify the target impact: Ask *"How much change is expected?"* and establish baseline vs. target metrics (e.g., One Metric That Matters or Overall Evaluation Criterion) [41-44].

---

##### 3. Output Artifacts

Depending on your team's methodology, summarize the transformed feedback into one of these standard output artifacts:

1. **Problem Statement Template**:
   * **For Existing Products**: 
     > *"[Our service/product] is intended to achieve [these goals]. We have observed that the product/service isn't meeting [these goals], which is causing [adverse effect] to our business. How might we improve [service/product] so that our customers are more successful based on [these measurable criteria]?"* [45]
   * **How Might We (HMW) Design Challenge**: 
     > *"How might we help [target persona] do [functional goal/need] in [context] so that [desired outcome/benefit] is achieved?"* [33, 46, 47]

2. **Behavioral Statement (Intervention Design)**:
   > *"When [population] wants to [motivation], and they [limitations], they will [behavior] (as measured by [data])."* [48]

3. **Behavioral User Story**:
   > *"In order to [desired behavior change / measurable benefit], as a [persona], I want to [new capability] (whereas currently [current friction/workaround])."* [36, 40, 49]

4. **Problem-Solution Canvas / Value Proposition Canvas**:
   * A structured canvas capturing the **Customer Profile** (Customer Jobs, Pains, Gains) alongside hypothesized **Pain Relievers** and **Gain Creators** [50-53].

## store-phase-1-triage-2: How are related stories and epics searched in the backlog (I2)?

- asked 2026-10-04 · notebook "The Product - Business Design" · conversation `d8f5ab7e-40e9-4461-8e9b-afff2eabe8e6`
- Sources: Adzic et al., Fifty Quick Ideas to Improve Your User Stories (2014); Kalbach, Mapping Experiences (2020); Patton, User Story Mapping; Humble et al., Lean Enterprise; Richter, The Product Manager's Playbook (2023); Cohn, User Stories Applied (2004)
- Note: cited passages abridged (first part of each passage, "…" marks cuts); answer text verbatim

**Question**

> Describe the step-by-step procedure for searching a product backlog for stories and epics related to a new piece of feedback, as the sources document it — where to look (story map, impact map, epics, open/in-progress/done stories, living documentation), the ordered steps to find duplicates, overlaps and the matching epic or user activity, and the output. Please cite the sources.

**Answer** (verbatim, citation markers resolve in the table below)

Based on the product management and agile frameworks documented in the sources, searching a product backlog for stories and epics related to a new piece of user feedback follows a structured procedure across hierarchical backlogs and visual planning artifacts [1-3].

---

##### 1. Where to Look

To conduct a comprehensive search, look across five primary locations in your backlog ecosystem:

1. **User Story Map**:
   * **Backbone (User Activities & Tasks)**: The top horizontal axis representing high-level user activities and workflow steps [1, 4, 5].
   * **Release Slices / Vertical Grids**: The vertical columns under each activity step, organized into prioritized release slices (e.g., current release, next release, or 'not now') [1, 5-7].
2. **Impact Map**:
   * A four-level mind map connecting **Business Goals** \\(\rightarrow\\) **Actors/User Segments** \\(\rightarrow\\) **Impacts (desired behavior changes)** \\(\rightarrow\\) **Deliverables (Epics/User Stories)** [3, 8].
3. **Opportunity / Product Discovery Backlog**:
   * Kanban boards tracking high-level opportunities across stages such as *Opportunity*, *In Discovery/Problem Research*, *In Ideation*, *In Delivery*, and *In Validation* [9].
4. **Product Delivery Backlog & Sprint Task Boards**:
   * Software development task boards (e.g., Jira) organized by active workflow columns: *Backlog* (unplanned), *Open* (allocated to sprint), *In Progress / Doing*, *Code Review*, and *QA* [10].
5. **Living Documentation & System Specifications**:
   * Specifications, acceptance tests, and feature maps organized strictly by **functional area** representing completed (*Done / Live*) functionality, rather than past story history [11, 12].

---

##### 2. Ordered Step-by-Step Procedure

When evaluating a new piece of feedback, execute the following steps in sequence:

###### **Step 1: Map Feedback to User Context & Intent**
* **Identify the Persona/Actor & Activity**: Determine which user role or customer segment the feedback represents [3, 13, 14].
* **Locate the Map Position**: Locate the corresponding high-level user activity step on the **Story Map backbone** [1, 5, 15] or the relevant actor and behavior change on the **Impact Map** [3]. Focusing on the problem statement or behavior change prevents creating premature, isolated solution cards [16].

###### **Step 2: Check Living Documentation (Verify "Done / Live" Features)**
* Search the **living documentation** and functional test suites to verify if the feedback pertains to functionality that has already been shipped [11, 17].
* *Why*: Once stories are completed, cards are discarded and system capabilities are documented by functional areas; checking here prevents attempting to re-implement existing behavior [11].

###### **Step 3: Search Active & Planned Sprints ("Open", "In Progress", "QA")**
* Search active task boards (*Open*, *In Progress*, *Code Review*, *QA*) and the topmost release slice on the story map [7, 10].
* Check for exact duplicates or active tasks currently addressing the feedback [10, 18, 19].

###### **Step 4: Search Unscheduled Backlogs, Opportunity Boards & Epics**
* Look through the unscheduled delivery backlog, open **Epics**, and the **Opportunity / Discovery Backlog** [9, 20, 21].
* Scan for broader themes or larger "rocks" (epics) where this feedback naturally fits as a sub-task or acceptance criterion [21, 22].

###### **Step 5: Identify Duplicates, Overlaps, or Scope Gaps**
* **Check for Duplicates**: Compare user role and intent to see if an identical story card exists; consolidate or discard redundant cards [18, 19].
* **Check for Overlaps**: Identify stories that share underlying technical or functional work [23]. Cluster related small items together [21].
* **Check for Scope Fit**: If using an Impact Map, verify if the feedback aligns visually with an existing branch (goal/impact); if it does not fit any branch, it is flagged as scope creep or 'not now' [13, 24].

---

##### 3. The Output

Following this procedure produces one of four explicit backlog outputs:

1. **Consolidated / Updated Open Story**:
   * If an active or planned story covers the request, update its notes, acceptance criteria, or questions rather than creating a new ticket [21, 23, 25].
2. **Distilled Epic or Theme (Clustered Stories)**:
   * If multiple overlapping stories or items exist, cluster them into a single higher-level parent story or epic title card, moving individual details into bullet points within the description [21].
3. **New Mapped Story Card**:
   * If the feedback represents a new, valid requirement, create a new story card [3, 5]. Position it under the matching **User Activity** step on the Story Map [1, 5] or **Impact branch** [3], and assign it to the appropriate release slice or opportunity stage [6, 9, 26].
4. **Discarded or Deferred Action**:
   * If the request is already fulfilled in the live system, mark it as resolved [9, 11]. If it fails to align with current milestone goals or user segments, assign it to the *Trash* or *Not Now* category [9, 13].

## store-phase-1-triage-3: Which pitfalls and terms apply to capturing feedback as a problem and searching the backlog (I1/I2)?

- asked 2026-10-04 · notebook "The Product - Business Design" · conversation `0bb2f354-1e52-4618-9753-348e71648f63`
- Sources: Product School, The Product Book (2017); Klein, UX for Lean Startups; Adzic et al., Fifty Quick Ideas to Improve Your User Stories (2014); Gothelf & Seiden, Lean UX; Wallaert, Start at the End (2019); Croll & Yoskovitz, Lean Analytics; Cohn, User Stories Applied (2004); Patton, User Story Mapping; LeMay, Product Management in Practice (2022); Lewrick et al., The Design Thinking Toolbox (2020); Kalbach, Mapping Experiences (2020); Richter, The Product Manager's Playbook (2023); Olsen, The Lean Product Playbook; Kelly, The Art of Agile Product Ownership (2019); Humble et al., Lean Enterprise

**Question**

> What mistakes or failure modes do the sources call out when capturing user feedback as a problem statement and when searching the backlog for related stories (e.g. solution wishes, duplicates, missing context, feedback treated at face value, stale backlog)? And which terms used there (e.g. problem statement, behaviour change, opportunity, epic, user activity, impact map, story map) would a generic reader not already know, and how do the sources define them? Please cite the sources.

**Answer** (verbatim, citation markers resolve in the table below)

##### 1. Mistakes and Failure Modes Called Out in the Sources

###### A. Capturing User Feedback as a Problem Statement

* **Taking Feature Requests at "Face Value"**: Customers frequently articulate their needs as specific solution wishes (e.g., requesting a "faster horse", "comments on product pages", or "database query optimization") [1-3]. A major failure mode is writing down "Users want feature X" without asking *why* or discovering the underlying pain point [4, 5].
* **Letting Customers Design the Product**: Accepting user solution wishes directly effectively forces users to design the software [4]. Because users rarely understand technical architecture, system constraints, or modern design patterns, this practice leads to overcomplicated, buggy, and expensive solutions that create long-term technical debt [6].
* **Disguising Solution Requests as Problem Statements**: Organizations often frame internal feature demands or competitor mimicry as business problem statements (e.g., "Our competitors shipped mobile apps, so we must build one") [7]. These are hidden requirement requests rather than true problem descriptions [7].
* **Reverse-Engineering Stories**: A common pitfall is starting with a deliverable ("I want..."), adding a plausible justification ("In order to..."), and tacking on a generic user segment ("As a user...") [8]. This "stream of consciousness" approach produces solution-centric tasks instead of addressing real user problems or driving actual behavior change [8, 9].
* **Ignoring Feedback Sampling Bias**: User feedback is heavily subject to sampling bias because tepid, normal experiences produce no feedback; people reach out primarily when they are ecstatic or furious [10]. Furthermore, users do not know their strategic value to the business and expect immediate action on their personal grievances [10].

---

###### B. Searching and Managing the Backlog for Related Stories

* **The Flat Backlog Trap ("Story Card Hell")**: Maintaining a flat list of hundreds of unorganized story cards causes teams to lose context and fail to comprehend what stories actually mean [11]. Searching through massive flat backlogs creates the illusion of iterative planning while obscuring the overall product vision [11].
* **Duplicate or Overlapping Feature Requests**: Small stories or individual feature requests often describe overlapping work (e.g., saving search results to XML vs. HTML) [12]. When unorganized, duplicate stories create conflicting estimates and redundant development efforts [12, 13].
* **Using Past Stories as Permanent Documentation**: Relying on completed stories to document current system state requires searching through history in reverse chronological order to deduce functionality [14]. The sources compare this to looking at a history of credit card receipts instead of your current account balance—it is error-prone, labor-intensive, and unsustainable [14].
* **Stale Backlogs and "Rotten Fruit"**: Backlogs easily become dumping grounds for untested, company-centric, or expired ideas [15, 16]. Without "best before" / expiration dates or regular pruning, time-sensitive stories quietly decay until they suddenly trigger self-inflicted emergencies [16, 17].
* **Uncoordinated "Stream of Consciousness" Backlogs**: Accepting stakeholder feature requests without a shared visual or hierarchical structure leads to an uncoordinated backlog where conflicting priorities cause constant context switching and slow down delivery [18].

---

##### 2. Key Terms and Their Source Definitions

* **Problem Statement**: An explicit description of a real customer pain point, need, or context written from the user's perspective without specifying a software implementation or feature solution [2, 7, 19]. In Design Thinking, it frames *what* the problem is, *who* experiences it, *why* it is a problem, and *when/where* it occurs [20].
* **Behaviour Change (or Behavioral Statement)**: An outcome articulated as a measurable change in human action (e.g., "get a profit overview faster" or "resolve customer data discrepancies faster") rather than a software deliverable [19, 21]. It defines the intended counterfactual world and serves as the core evaluation metric for product interventions [9, 21].
* **Opportunity**: An unfulfilled customer need, pain point, or gap where value can be created [22-24]. Quantitatively, it is defined as the maximum potential customer value that can be added: \\(\text{Opportunity Score} = \text{Importance} \times (1 - \text{Satisfaction})\\) [25, 26]. In backlog management, an "opportunity backlog" stores validated problems prior to committing to concrete solutions or delivery features [27, 28].
* **Epic**: A large user story containing significant scope and uncertainty that must be broken down into smaller, manageable stories before it can be scheduled into a development iteration [29-32]. Epics represent the right level of abstraction for business or user discussions, but are too large for development [33].
* **User Activity**: High-level workflow steps or overarching customer goals (e.g., "Purchasing a book" or "Organizing email") that aggregate multiple individual user tasks [34-36]. User activities form the horizontal "backbone" of a story map [35-37].
* **Impact Map**: A visual mind-mapping technique created by Gojko Adzic that connects technical deliverables to high-level business goals across four levels: Goal (Why?), Actor (Who?), Impact / Behavior Change (How?), and Deliverable / Story (What?) [38-40].
* **Story Map (User Story Mapping)**: A visual backlog organization framework invented by Jeff Patton [41]. It arranges high-level user activities chronologically along a horizontal axis ("backbone") and aligns detailed user stories vertically under each activity step by delivery priority and release milestones [34, 37, 42].
