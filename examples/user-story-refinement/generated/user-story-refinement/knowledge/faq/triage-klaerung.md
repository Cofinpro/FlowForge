## triage-klaerung-1: What do the sources add to intake, triage and business clarification, and what are the gateway criteria?

- asked 2026-09-26 · notebook "The Product - Business Design" · conversation `1b97c02c-6d96-4167-9231-8cdcca270660`
- Sources: Patton, User Story Mapping; Richter, The Product Manager's Playbook (2023); Adzic et al., Fifty Quick Ideas to Improve Your User Stories (2014); Evans, Domain-Driven Design (2003); Cohn, User Stories Applied (2004); Anforderungsvalidierung.pdf; Pohl & Rupp, Basiswissen Requirements Engineering (2015); Gothelf & Seiden, Lean UX; Bland, Testing Business Ideas (2019); Olsen, The Lean Product Playbook; Klein, UX for Lean Startups; Lewrick et al., The Design Thinking Toolbox (2020); The Design Thinking Playbook; LeMay, Product Management in Practice (2022)

**Question**

> Context: a running IT delivery project with an existing product vision, epics, a Fachkonzept (business concept with domain model) and a product backlog. A new user story starts from user or stakeholder feedback. Our process for the first two phases is: (1) Intake & triage: capture feedback and phrase it as a problem (not a solution wish) -> search related stories and epics in the backlog -> gateway 'malfunction of an existing function?' (yes -> hand to bug handling) -> gateway 'how does the request relate to the backlog?' (change to an open story -> hand over there; no epic relation -> park in opportunity backlog; otherwise new story under an existing epic). (2) Business clarification: clarify the request with the feedback giver (human step) -> check against the epic goal and Fachkonzept -> gateway 'compatible with the Fachkonzept?' (no -> request a Fachkonzept change, end) -> in parallel: PO describes the as-is/to-be delta, UX identifies affected screens and flows, development gives a rough technical impact estimate -> gateway 'relevant uncertainty open?' (technical -> time-boxed spike 1-3 days; value/usability -> clickable prototype test with 3-5 users; else continue) -> human step 'evaluate findings' -> gateway 'do the findings support the story?' (no -> discard). Based on the sources: (a) Which steps, checks, roles or artifacts do the sources describe for these two phases that this process lacks or does differently? (b) What are the concrete pass/fail criteria for the three gateways 'compatible with the Fachkonzept?', 'relevant uncertainty open?' and 'do the findings support the story?' (c) Which pitfalls do the sources name for these phases, and would this process catch them? Please cite the sources.

**Answer** (verbatim, citation markers resolve in the table below)

Analyzing your proposed two-phase intake and clarification process against the literature in your notebook reveals strong alignments—such as enforcing problem phrasing and time-boxing research—as well as several critical gaps, missing artifacts/roles, and rigidities highlighted by agile and requirements engineering experts.

---

##### (a) Missing or Differently Described Steps, Checks, Roles, and Artifacts

1. **Multi-Level Backlog Architecture & Triaging Frameworks**:
   * **Missing Artifacts/Steps**: Your process routes non-epic stories to an "opportunity backlog" [1]. The sources describe a more structured 3-tier backlog system: an **Idea Backlog** (raw, unassessed suggestions), an **Opportunity / Discovery Backlog** (initiatives undergoing problem/solution validation), and a **Delivery Backlog** (sprint-ready stories in Jira) [2-4]. 
   * **Triaging Method**: Rather than simple relational categorization, sources recommend formal triaging frameworks like the **Purpose Alignment Model** (asking: *Is it mission-critical? Is it market-differentiating?*) to categorize items into Partner, Differentiate, Parity, or Who Cares [5, 6].

2. **Iterative Model Refactoring vs. Rigid "Fachkonzept" Gating**:
   * **Different Mindset**: In Domain-Driven Design (DDD), checking compatibility with a domain model ("Fachkonzept") is not a static one-way gate (*"compatible? no \\(\rightarrow\\) request change, end"*) [7, 8]. Encoountering incompatibilities or new terminology during clarification is a **"knowledge crunching"** trigger to iteratively refactor the **Ubiquitous Language** and the domain model together with domain experts [7, 9-11]. Treating the domain model as a rigid pre-existing document creates a "deadly divide" between analysis and design [12, 13].
   * **Formal RE Validation**: Requirements Engineering (IREB / Pohl & Rupp) specifies that validation must check three distinct quality aspects: **Content** (completeness/correctness), **Documentation** (compliance with rules), and **Agreement/Consensus** (resolving stakeholder conflicts) [14, 15].

3. **Defining Measurable Outcomes & Behavior Changes (Not Just As-Is/To-Be Deltas)**:
   * **Missing Step**: Your process has the Product Owner (PO) describe an "as-is/to-be delta". Sources emphasize that stories must articulate explicit, quantifiable **target behavior changes** (*"In order to..."*) and measurable outcomes [16-18]. Without defining how user behavior will change or setting a target metric, you cannot establish meaningful pass/fail criteria for validation [17, 18].

4. **Collaborative Discovery ("3 Amigos") vs. Parallel Work**:
   * **Missing Roles/Workflows**: Phase 2 runs PO, UX, and Development work "in parallel" as separate tasks. Sources advocate for **collaborative story refinement workshops** bringing together the **"Customer Team" / "3 Amigos"** (Product Manager/PO, UX/Designer, Developers, and **QA/Testers**) [19-21].
   * **Missing Role**: **QA / Testers** are notably absent from your clarification phase. Cohn explicitly states that testers are essential during clarification to help specify acceptance tests on the back of story cards before coding [19, 21, 22].

5. **Non-Functional Requirements (NFRs) & Cross-Cutting Constraints**:
   * **Missing Check**: The process focuses on functional deltas, UX screens, and technical spikes. Sources explicitly mandate reviewing **quality attributes and constraints** (e.g., performance, security, capacity, supportability via FURPS+ or Quality Pyramids) during clarification, as individual user stories frequently impact global system constraints [23-26].

6. **Drafting Acceptance Criteria / Tests Upfront**:
   * **Missing Artifact**: Drafting concrete **acceptance criteria / tests** (using FitNesse tables, scenario checklists, or Given-When-Then formats) during clarification is crucial to align expectations between stakeholders and developers before development or spikes begin [22, 27, 28].

---

##### (b) Concrete Pass/Fail Criteria for the Three Gateways

###### 1. Gateway: *"Compatible with the Fachkonzept?"*
* **Pass Criteria**:
  * **Unification & Consistency**: The request uses terms consistent with the project's **Ubiquitous Language** and does not introduce contradictory business rules or false cognates [11, 29-32].
  * **Invariant & Context Preservation**: The story respects the core domain invariants and stays within the defined **Bounded Context** boundaries without breaking model integrity [29, 33, 34].
  * **Strategic Alignment**: The request aligns with the overarching **Domain Vision Statement** and business goals [35, 36].
* **Fail Criteria**:
  * The story introduces fundamental logical contradictions into the domain rules that cannot be reconciled without splitting the model unmanageably [30, 32, 33].
  * It forces pre-mature design decisions or architecture violations that break the domain's conceptual contours [32, 37].

###### 2. Gateway: *"Relevant uncertainty open?"*
* **For Technical Spikes**:
  * **Pass (Uncertainty Resolved)**: The spike satisfies its pre-defined acceptance criteria within its strict time-box; code proves feasibility; the team can estimate complexity and performance impact [38, 39].
  * **Fail (Uncertainty Remains)**: The time-box expires without achieving acceptance criteria, performance/scalability barriers remain unresolved, or the required technical effort exceeds sustainable thresholds [38-40].
* **For Value / Usability Prototype Tests**:
  * **Pass (Uncertainty Resolved)**: Qualitative testing with 3–5 target persona users yields high completion rates across key scenarios without fatal confusion [41-44]; quantitative validation scores meet target thresholds (e.g., median Value and Ease-of-Use ratings \\(\ge 8/10\\) across iterative waves) [95, 98–101].
  * **Fail (Uncertainty Remains)**: More than 40–60% of test users fail core tasks, express confusion over value propositions, or complain about missing baseline capabilities [94–97, 110].

###### 3. Gateway: *"Do the findings support the story?"*
* **Pass Criteria**:
  * **Desirability & Fit**: User/solution interviews and prototype testing confirm that target customers actually experience the problem and actively desire the proposed solution [45-47].
  * **Feasibility & Performance**: Technical evaluation confirms the solution can be delivered within acceptable cost, maintainability, and architectural bounds [38, 48].
  * **Viability & Behavior Impact**: Evidence demonstrates that the story will drive the desired behavior change or business outcome without generating excessive technical debt or maintenance overhead [16, 17, 49].
* **Fail Criteria**:
  * **Falsified Hypothesis**: Testing reveals low user demand, proving the feature is an unneeded "white elephant" or solution in search of a problem [4, 50, 51].
  * **Unjustified Complexity**: The spike/prototype shows that solving the problem requires disproportionate technical effort relative to the customer value delivered [38, 48, 52].
