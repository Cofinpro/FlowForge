## Product-Methodik: Fachliche Klärung

_Ort: notebook:The Product - Business Design_

Short conversation of the PO with the feedback giver to confirm the problem behind a wish. This is a human
checkpoint: Claude prepares the questions and a draft problem statement, the PO talks to the giver and
decides when the problem is confirmed. The notebook composes the procedure from interview and requirements
sources; no source describes a step by this name, and the sources are written for customer or stakeholder
interviews, so applying them to a feedback giver is a transfer. The gate criteria for this step ("problem
confirmed", "expected outcome and urgency known") are in the spec; the gateway criteria that follow are
in `phase-2-fachliche-klaerung.md`.

**Starting point.** Feedback usually arrives as a solution, not a problem [^1][^2]. The goal of the step is
to turn the request into a user problem statement through a quick (about five-minute) clarification
[feedback-driven-stories-1: 11, 23], using the questions below [store-phase-2-klaerung-4].

**Questions to prepare** [store-phase-2-klaerung-4: 3-14, 16, 17]:

- Why: ask "why" repeatedly (Five Whys, benefit laddering) to reach the real need [^3][^4]; "Wozu genau
  brauchen wir das?" / "Was verbessert sich dadurch konkret?" [^5].
- Problem, actor, context, workaround: what is the problem and why, who has it, when and where does it occur,
  how is it solved today [^6]; what was the giver doing right before, how often [^7].
- Impact and urgency: how many users are affected, effect on company goals, what happens if it waits two weeks
  versus six months [^8].
- Expected outcome: ask for the behaviour change, not the software feature [^9].

**Confirming the problem, not the solution** [store-phase-2-klaerung-4: 13, 22-25, 28, 30]:

1. Mirror, paraphrase and summarise what the giver said [^10]; at the end summarise expectations and needs
   back to them [^11].
2. Write a solution-neutral problem statement, not a "lack-of-solution" statement [^12], or the job form
   "When (situation), I want to (motivation), so I can (expected result)" [^13].
3. Test the root cause with reverse questions [^14].
4. Walk through concrete examples; agreement on three to four difficult boundary conditions is a sign of
   shared understanding [^15].

**Recording the answer** [store-phase-2-klaerung-4: 25, 33-37, 39]:

- Annotate the story card or ticket briefly; the conversation matters more than the note [^16].
- Use the usual backlog attributes: problem description or justification, acceptance criterion, stated
  solution-neutral and concrete [^17].
- Two people for a dedicated session, one leads, one documents [^18]; keep wording verbatim, note emotional
  utterances, and write a "maybe" down as "no" [^19].

**When to stop** [store-phase-2-klaerung-4: 13, 30, 40, 41]:

- Stop asking "why" once it no longer yields new insight; five is no guarantee, so ask until the real cause
  shows [^20][^21].
- Close with "Is there anything else about this topic that I should have asked?" [^22].
- Agreement on the boundary conditions ends the conversation [^15].
- Time-boxing: the notebook cites time boxes (10 to 15 minutes) for story discussions, not for feedback calls [^23].

**Pitfalls the notebook names** [store-phase-2-klaerung-4: 47-56]:

- Accepting the requested solution at face value; not questioning an incoming requirement [^24].
- Leading or closed questions ("do you agree that...") cause acquiescence bias [^25]; use open,
  context-free questions [^26].
- Asking what the giver would do in future instead of what happened [^27].
- Talking more than listening, pitching the solution [^28]; designing screens in the conversation [^29].

**Not answered by the sources.** No fixed question script or checklist for a feedback giver (support ticket,
review, sprint review); no time limit for this clarification beyond the "about five minutes" in the
notebook's own answer; no format for the clarification record beyond card annotations and requirement
attributes; nothing on a giver who cannot be reached.
