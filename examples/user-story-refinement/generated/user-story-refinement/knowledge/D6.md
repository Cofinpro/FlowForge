## Product-Methodik: Refinement

_Ort: notebook:The Product - Business Design_

**Task: "Story vertikal schneiden (Splitting-Muster)"** (PO). Runs when the gateway "Passt die Story
in einen Sprint?" says no; the part stories are then reformulated. Sizing criteria (about 1-5 ideal
days, within one sprint's velocity) and the short pattern list are in `phase-4-refinement.md`
(FAQ `refinement-1`, (b)/(c)); this file adds the examples, split checks and pitfalls.

### Rule: vertical, never by layer

Each part story gets a little of every layer and gives end-to-end value ("slicing the cake"). A split
into "fill out the form" and "write it to the database" leaves two stories that are useless on
their own [^1][^2].

### Patterns, with the sources' examples

- **Compound story, by capability:** "A user can post her resume" becomes add/edit education, job
  history, salary history, publications … [^3]
- **By business rule / data variation:** one credit card type first; search by author or title,
  then by publication name or date, then combined [^4][^5]. Photo sharing by channel (Facebook, email
  …) [^6].
- **Spike, then feature (learning vs. earning):** "Investigate credit card processing over the web"
  (time-boxed, with its own goal), then "A user can pay with a credit card" [^7][^8].
- **Outputs before inputs:** a daily seven-day summary report for one activity type, without
  authentication, custom periods or on-demand runs; hard-coded currency list before the database
  connection [^9][^10]. A simplified output (files instead of the data warehouse) only works if end
  users value it [^11].
- **Narrow the customer segment:** two operators handling UK trades (no tax complexity) first [^12].
- **Extract basic utility:** a borderline-usable tool in two days for a regulator deadline; tell
  stakeholders upfront [^13][^14].
- **Examples of usefulness:** pick the few usage examples that need only a small part of the solution
  [^15].
- **Hamburger:** list the workflow steps (assemble recipients, send, get status, handle bounces),
  list options per step, take one thin slice across all steps [^16]. **Walking skeleton:** a thin
  slice that cuts through all functionality so it runs end to end [^17].

### Checks for a good split

Each part is valuable to users or customers, independent, estimable, small and testable (INVEST) [^18];
its estimate lies below the maximum threshold [^19]. The notebook's guidance for choosing a pattern
(its synthesis of the passages above, no single quote): spike when driven by technical uncertainty;
segment or channel when driven by roles or platforms; simplified outputs or hard-coded reference
data when driven by data and legacy integration; basic utility under deadline; hamburger or skeleton
when stuck in all-or-nothing thinking [^20].

### Part stories afterwards

The customer prioritizes the parts separately, e.g. editing credit cards now, addresses a few
iterations later [^21]. If parts become too small to be worth estimating, combine them again [^22].
Keep the hierarchy, so the parts are measured against the original business goal [^23].

### Pitfalls

- Layer splits, see the rule above [^2].
- Fake and micro stories: stories about delivery-team needs, or pieces so small they carry no risk
  [^23].
- Splitting often during planning is a smell; refinement came too late [^24].
- Always taking the easy slice and skipping the risky one ("children choosing chocolate over
  broccoli") [^25]. Basic-utility slices without stakeholder alignment can backfire [^14].
