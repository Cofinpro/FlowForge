**Task: "Story Points schätzen (Planning Poker)"** (Entwicklung / Tech Lead, human checkpoint). The
developers estimate; Claude can prepare the story, the reference stories and the open questions, but
proposes no number of its own. The convergence rule (up to 2-3 rounds) and the sprint-size criteria are
in `phase-4-refinement.md`; this file adds how the round works, what happens
with a story that is too big or too uncertain, and what is recorded.

### Who does what

- The developers who build and test the story estimate, and they own the estimate as a team, not as
  individuals [^1].
- The PO presents the story and answers questions. She takes part while the programmers estimate but may
  not give her own estimate or editorialize about an estimate she dislikes [^2]. If an estimate is far
  off, she clarifies, e.g. that she is asking for something much simpler [^3].

### Reference stories and scale

- Keep a reference set of stories of known sizes and compare new stories with it; this helps estimate
  scope more accurately [^4].
- Triangulate: a story estimated at four points should be about twice the size of a two-point story, a
  three-pointer larger than the two and smaller than the four. This checks that the team is not
  gradually changing the meaning of a point. Pinning cards to a wall in columns labelled with the points
  is one way to do it [^5].
- A point is a relative estimate of complexity, effort or duration [^6]. The team defines it; Cohn's own
  preference is an ideal day of work [^7].
- The scale is a small fixed set: Fibonacci (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 ...), which forces distinct differences
  [^8]; or ½, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20, 40, 80, which reflects that we know less about larger stories [^9].
  Powers of two and T-shirt sizes are also in use [^10].

### One round

1. The PO reads or presents the story and the developers ask questions [^11].
2. Each developer privately picks a card; everyone reveals at once [^4][^11].
3. If the cards differ materially, the high and the low estimator explain their thinking. This must not
   sound like an attack; the point is to learn what they considered (e.g. a mock database to build, a
   simpler storage option) [^12]. The group discusses for up to a few minutes, the PO clarifies, a note
   goes on the card, perhaps a new story is written; then everyone estimates again [^13].
4. Converged: in many cases already after round two; it rarely takes more than three rounds, and one
   continues only while the estimates move closer together. Identical cards are not required; with
   4, 4, 4 and 3 the low estimator is asked whether four is fine. The aim is reasonableness, not
   precision [^14]. Do not argue 7 versus 8 on large stories; that claims more precision than the team
   has [^15].

### When a story cannot be estimated

- **Too big:** a story like "A Job Seeker can find a job" is split into smaller stories first [^16].
  Stories at the high end of the scale have large scope and uncertainty and are broken down; epics
  must be broken down before they can enter a sprint [^17]. Patterns: `${CLAUDE_SKILL_DIR}/../story-refinement/references/D6.md`.
- **Lack of knowledge:** send one or more developers on a spike, a brief experiment with a defined
  maximum time (a timebox). The unestimatable story becomes two: a quick spike to gather information,
  then the story to do the real work [^18].
- Put the spike in a different iteration than the stories that depend on it; only the investigative
  story can be estimated, and the customer can prioritize the research separately [^19].

### What is recorded

- The estimate goes on the story card or into the tracking system next to the story [^20]; notes
  from the discussion are jotted on the card [^13].
- When a story is split, the estimates of the parts need not add up to the estimate of the original [^21].
- Velocity uses the points assigned before the iteration; they are not changed afterwards [^22].

### Pitfalls

- False precision: arguing between neighbouring large numbers [^15]; adding up story sizes into
  long-term forecasts, where the numbers look precise but are worse than guesses without rigorous
  statistics [^23]. Adzic et al. see sizing as useful for one purpose, deciding whether a story is too
  big or small enough for quick feedback, and warn against turning rough estimates into dated
  commitments [^24].
- Comparing points across teams: one team's three points may be another team's five [^21].
- Changing the points of a finished story because it turned out larger [^22].
- Letting a stakeholder or the PO influence the cards: the private pick with a simultaneous reveal
  follows from Olsen's description [^4]; LeMay uses the same "scrum poker" way of asking a question to
  minimize groupthink [^25].

### Not answered by the notebook

- Which story is a good reference story, and how many to keep: the sources only name "a reference set of
  user stories of different known sizes" [^4]. The notebook's "standard 1-point story" is not in the
  passages it cites.
- Which scale to choose, and a numeric maximum above which a story must be split: the sources say "high
  end of the scale" [^17], not a number (sizing in days: `phase-4-refinement.md`).
- What to do when the estimates still diverge after three rounds: the sources say only "rarely more than
  three" [^14]; the loop cap of 3 rounds in the diagram is a design decision.
- A fixed record format beyond the card or tracking tool [^20].
- The notebook's pitfall "converting points directly to hours" is not backed by the passages it cites;
  Cohn treats a point as ideal time that can be converted later [^7], and his rule is that velocity
  does not use actual hours [^22]. The notebook's "PO strictly forbidden to gasp" is a paraphrase of
  "not allowed to ... editorialize" [^2].
