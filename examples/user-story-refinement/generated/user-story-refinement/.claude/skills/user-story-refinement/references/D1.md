**Task: "Refinement mit Three Amigos ansetzen"** (PO, human checkpoint). The PO schedules the
refinement; Claude can prepare the invitation and the material, the people decide. Roles, inputs and
outputs of the session itself are in `phase-4-refinement.md` (step 1); this file adds who to invite, what to have ready, the time-box and when to
postpone.

### Who attends

- One person each from business/analysis, development and testing; four or five is fine if another
  role is needed (e.g. a DBA or a domain expert) [^1]. Typical line-up: the PO, the programmer(s) who
  will develop the story, and the tester [^2]. A good size is three to five people [^3].
- Invite the people who will actually build and test the story, not whoever has time; otherwise special
  cases have to be discussed again in iteration planning [^4].
- Announce beforehand which stories will be discussed (wall or broadcast) and let team members opt in
  [^5]. Not everybody belongs in every conversation; small groups of two to five decide best [^6].

### What must be ready beforehand

- The story card, a placeholder for the conversation [^2].
- A few initial scenarios of how the business side sees the story working; the session starts with
  them [^7].
- Discovery material where it exists: UI prototypes, architectural or technical design sketches [^8].
- A slot that fits the scarcest role. If one role is the bottleneck (e.g. only one business
  representative), plan the conversations upfront around it, e.g. a one-hour slot per developer/tester
  pair each Monday [^9]. If most stories need coordination with stakeholders or third-party teams,
  schedule the discussion about one cycle ahead [^10].

### Agenda and time-box

1. The analyst or business representative introduces the story and the initial scenarios. The developer
   checks it against the existing infrastructure and probes for functional gaps and inconsistencies.
   The tester asks how it could be tested and applies testing heuristics for scenarios the others missed.
   It continues until everyone has enough information to start and the major risks are covered [^7].
2. Make the understanding visible with three to four very simple concrete examples on a whiteboard or
   flipchart; boundary conditions come naturally after that. The aim is good questions quickly, not all
   the answers [^11].
3. Time-box. One team's policy: three hours for the whole session, at most two cycles of 20 minutes per
   story, which covers three to six stories for a two-week iteration; after the first block the team
   moves on if everyone is clear [^12]. Another rule of thumb: pause a story after 30 minutes and decide
   whether more analysis is worth it [^13]. The diverge-and-merge cycles themselves are described for
   teams of 10 to 20 split into groups [^14]; for three to five people the sources give only the
   time-box numbers.

### Outputs

- Agreed acceptance criteria [^15]; the story split into "right-sized" stories of one to three days to
  build and test where needed [^15].
- Key boundary conditions and open questions [^12].
- Written up by one of the amigos when working on a flipchart or whiteboard, shared with the team;
  around a monitor or with screen-sharing the participants write the notes directly [^16].

### When to postpone, escalate or split

- **Escalate:** a major impact on the current architecture or a globally significant feature change;
  pause and continue with the whole team [^17].
- **Still too vague after the second 20-minute block:** the PO either splits the story or takes it out
  for detailed analysis. Split if the team can agree on a part that is worth releasing separately (main
  scenarios clear, exceptions need business clarification). Take it out if the team could not discuss or
  agree on the parts with significant business risk [^18].
- **Cannot even be grasped well enough to split in 30 minutes:** consider postponing it to the next
  iteration to allow time for detailed analysis [^19].

### Pitfalls

- Delegating the analysis to people who will not deliver the story [^4], or to just one person [^1].
- Everyone in every conversation; people who are not interested make it harder [^6].
- Letting one story eat the time-box; see the rules above [^13][^18].

### Not answered by the notebook

- A fixed duration for a single three-amigos meeting: the numbers above are one team's policy [^12] or
  rules of thumb [^13], not a prescription.
- How far ahead of sprint planning the meeting must happen, beyond "immediately before a story is to be
  developed" [^2] and "about one cycle ahead" where coordination is heavy [^10].
- A checklist of entry criteria for the meeting (the Definition of Ready is a separate phase, later in the flow);
  what to have ready is the condensed list above, from passages that name the items one by one.
- Whether a postponed story may be postponed repeatedly; no limit is given.
